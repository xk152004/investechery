Section 2

What the company does

The problem Visa solves is trust between strangers who will never meet. A merchant in Lisbon has no way to know whether a card presented by a visitor from Seoul is good for the money, and no practical way to collect if it is not. Visa's answer is a rulebook every participating bank agrees to in advance, a switch that carries the authorisation to the issuing bank and back in under a second, and a settlement guarantee that pays the merchant even where the issuer subsequently fails. What is sold is not technology. It is the certainty that the payment will complete.

The structure that delivers it is the four-party model, and its shape determines everything downstream. The cardholder pays the merchant in full. The merchant's acquiring bank credits the merchant that amount less a merchant discount rate. Out of that rate, the acquirer remits interchange to the card-issuing bank and network fees to Visa; the issuer separately pays Visa its own network fees out of the interchange it received. Visa is therefore paid by both sides of its own platform, on a transaction it neither funded nor underwrote.

Follow one transaction

A cardholder taps. The acquirer routes the authorisation to VisaNet, which applies the issuer's rules and Visa's own risk scoring and returns an approval. Visa earns a data processing fee on that switch, from both banks, recognised in the period the transaction occurs. Separately it earns a service fee assessed on the payments volume carried on Visa credentials — billed in arrears, which is why service revenue in any quarter is calculated on the prior quarter's volume. If the issuer sits in a different country from the merchant, the same transaction also generates international transaction revenue, covering cross-border processing and the currency conversion inside it. Clearing and settlement follow within days; Visa moves the funds but never owns them.

What has been added to the same transaction

The material change to the business model over the last decade is that Visa stopped selling only the switch. Value-added services — issuing and acceptance solutions, risk and fraud tooling, advisory and marketing — generated $10.9bn in FY2025, up 24%, and $3.8bn in the June 2026 quarter alone, up 33%. On a last-twelve-months basis that is roughly 30% of net revenue against 22% in FY2023 (inferred). They are sold to the same banks and merchants, on the same transactions, through the same contracts. They require no additional volume to grow, and no regulator anywhere has capped their price.

Visa Direct points the other way — outward, to transactions that are not card purchases. It moves money to approximately 12 billion endpoints across more than 195 countries, using over 90 domestic payment schemes and more than 60 card and wallet networks, and processed more than 12.5 billion transactions for over 650 partners in FY2025. Its significance is that it puts Visa on rails it does not own, including the account-to-account rails below — converting a substitution threat into a distribution channel. Revenue is not disclosed separately (NOT FOUND).

One move went the other way. In February 2026 Visa paid $1.5bn cash for Prisma Medios de Pago and Newpay in Argentina. Prisma is an acquirer and processor — a position below Visa in the value chain, where, as the next section shows, margins are roughly half the network's.

Section 3

Industry, competitive position and moat

The card network industry exists because bilateral trust does not scale. Without a scheme, every issuing bank would have to contract with every acquiring bank in every country, and the number of agreements grows as the square of the participants. A scheme replaces that with one rulebook and one switch. The industry is therefore structurally a two-sided platform, and two-sided platforms consolidate: the value of joining rises with the number already joined, so share, once won, is very hard to move.

The outside evidence says it has not moved. The BIS finds the combined global share of Visa and Mastercard in card transactions stable at approximately 95% for a decade, with revenues and margins growing through 2024 despite the proliferation of payment apps. In the UK, the Payment Systems Regulator finds the two carry more than 95% of transactions on UK-issued cards.

Where the profit pool sits

The most useful outside evidence is the Federal Reserve's measurement of the US debit chain, because it is the one jurisdiction where a regulator counts every dollar. In calendar 2023, 100.7bn debit and prepaid transactions moved $4.7 trillion. Issuers received $34.1bn of interchange, 0.73% of value. The networks received $12.9bn of network fees, 0.28% of value. Of that combined pool the network keeps roughly 27% and the issuer roughly 73% (inferred). The Reserve Bank of Australia reaches the same shape from a different angle: acquirer-side scheme fees averaged 10.5 basis points of transaction value in FY2024/25 and issuer-side fees 3.3 basis points — a total network take of about 13.8 basis points.

What actually binds as a barrier

Three barriers are usually cited and only one binds. Brand does not — Amex, Discover and JCB all have brands and none has scale. Technology does not — Pix and UPI were both built from nothing by central authorities in under a decade. What binds is the simultaneity requirement: an entrant must sign issuers and merchants at the same time, because neither side will join a network the other has not joined. Visa's disclosed reach is approximately 12 billion endpoints against more than 175 million merchant locations in more than 200 countries. No commercial entrant has ever assembled both sides at once. The two that came closest did it by government mandate, which is the point.

The second barrier is less discussed and, so far, more durable. Every regulator that has intervened has capped the issuer's cut and left the network's alone. The Durbin Amendment caps US debit interchange and says nothing about network fees. The EU Interchange Fee Regulation caps interchange at 0.2% on debit and 0.3% on credit and puts scheme fees explicitly out of scope. Australia, Brazil and India have each capped or eliminated interchange while leaving network fees unregulated.

US debit network fees have held at 0.28% of transaction value continuously from Regulation II's introduction in 2011 through 2023 — while the per-transaction network fee rose from $0.098 to $0.129. Federal Reserve, 2023 Interchange Fee Revenue, Covered Issuer Costs and Fraud Losses (published December 2025)

The European Commission measured the same thing from the other side: acquirers saved roughly €2.7bn in 2017 and issuers lost roughly €3.0bn a year, while the schemes gained approximately €550m a year from higher scheme fees between 2015 and 2017 — recouping about a fifth of the transfer within two years, through the one fee the regulation did not reach.

That asymmetry is now what regulators have noticed, which makes it the thing most likely to change. The PSR's March 2025 final report found average core scheme fees rose at least 25% in real terms between 2017 and 2023, costing UK businesses at least £170m a year more, with scheme margins higher than would be expected in competitive markets against comparators earning 12–18%. Its remedies, consulted on again in May 2026, are transparency and regulatory financial reporting — not price. The RBA reached the same conclusion in March 2026, declined to cap for want of evidence, required Scheme Fee Roadmaps by April 2027, and explicitly retained the option to intervene. Both regulators have now built the evidentiary base a cap would require.

The substitution question, and what the evidence shows

The genuine threat to a card network is not a better card network. It is a rail that moves money directly between bank accounts and bypasses the scheme entirely. Three have now scaled far enough to be tested against evidence rather than argued about.

India is the cleanest experiment, because the government set the merchant discount rate on UPI to zero in January 2020 and left credit card fees uncapped. Between calendar 2019 and 2024 UPI volume compounded at 74% a year. Over exactly the same period, Indian debit card volume fell at 18.9% a year while credit card volume grew at 16.5% and credit value at 23.4% a year (RBI Payment Systems Report). Brazil repeats it: Pix carried 79.8bn transactions worth R$35.3tn in 2025, yet Brazilian card value still grew 10.1% — because credit grew 15.2% and debit grew 0.0% (ABECS).

The mechanism is that a bank transfer and a card purchase only look alike at the moment of payment. A card purchase carries a credit line, a rewards accrual, a chargeback right and a merchant-acceptance guarantee. A push payment carries none of these — which is why it wins the low-value, immediate, no-recourse transaction that was cash or debit, and loses the discretionary one. Visa's exposure is concentrated in exactly the segment that is already the most regulated and the lowest-yielding.

What kind of company wins here is therefore specific: the one that owns the switch and refuses to own the credit. It earns a fraction of a percent of an enormous base, requires almost no capital to carry more of it, and sits in the one link of the chain regulators have consistently declined to price. Visa is that kind of company — more purely so than any listed comparable — and its margin is the highest in the chain precisely because it has the least to do with the money.

Section 4

Growth engine

Visa's growth decomposes cleanly because the company discloses the driver behind each revenue line. In FY2025 net revenue grew 11%. Service revenue rose 9% on payments volume growth of 7% plus pricing and card benefits. Data processing rose 13% on transaction growth of 10% plus pricing. International transaction rose 12% on cross-border volume growth of 13% excluding intra-Europe. Other rose 27% on advisory services plus pricing.

Two things follow. First, in every line the volume driver and the revenue outcome differ by two to three points, and the difference is disclosed as pricing — so Visa takes price annually, in modest increments, as a supplement to the engine rather than the engine itself. Second, transaction count is growing faster than payments volume (10% against 7%), meaning the average ticket is falling as cards displace cash at the low-value end. Because data processing revenue is assessed per transaction, this mix shift quietly moves the base toward the line that is indifferent to the price level.

Source: Visa Forms 10-K, FY2018, FY2020, FY2022 and FY2025. Volume compounded at 8.6% a year over the nine years (inferred) — and grew through the pandemic.

Reported versus organic

One acquisition is material enough to matter and it cannot be separated out. Visa bought Prisma and Newpay in February 2026 for $1.5bn cash and does not disclose the revenue contribution. The 14% and 15% growth rates for the June 2026 quarter and nine months are therefore reported, not organic, and the filings do not permit the decomposition. Two indirect readings bound it: the consideration is 3.4% of last-twelve-months net revenue of $44.5bn (inferred), and Visa attributes the revenue increase to volume and transactions while naming acquisitions only as a driver of personnel, network and professional-fee expense — which suggests the effect is larger on the cost line. This stays an open question.

The drivers, ranked

Structural
Cash displacement

The largest and slowest driver. Cash was still 52% of euro-area point-of-sale transactions by number in 2024 (ECB SPACE) and cash in circulation is around 9% of GDP in advanced economies (BIS). Every point of that conversion arrives on the existing network at essentially zero incremental cost. It asks nothing of management and it does not stop in a recession.

Management-driven
Value-added services attach

$10.9bn in FY2025, up 24%, and up 33% in the June 2026 quarter — roughly 30% of last-twelve-months net revenue against 22% in FY2023 (inferred). Sold into the installed base rather than won from competitors, and unregulated. The one growth lever genuinely inside management's control.

Cyclical, with a structural floor
Cross-border volume

Up 13% in FY2025 and 14–15% through the June 2026 quarter, on the highest-yielding line in the business. Cross-border ecommerce is the structural half; travel is the cyclical half, and the next section shows what it does in a downturn.

Management-driven, incremental
Annual pricing modifications

Worth roughly two to three points of growth per revenue line in FY2025. Real and repeatable — and the component most exposed to the scheme-fee scrutiny above.

Structural
Geographic mix

The US fell from 43% of net revenue in FY2023 to 39% in FY2025 — not because US revenue shrank (it grew 6%) but because international grew 15%. Growth is coming from the markets where cash share is highest: the first driver arriving through a different door.

Temporary and small
Acquisitions

$887m in FY2025 and $705m in the first nine months of FY2026, against $18.3bn of buybacks in FY2025. Prisma at $1.5bn is the largest in years and is still 3.4% of annual revenue. M&A is not how this business grows.

What is conspicuously absent is new customers. Visa does not grow by adding merchants or cardholders in any meaningful sense — it already reaches ~12 billion endpoints and 175m+ merchant locations. It grows because the transactions those endpoints already generate keep migrating onto its rails, and because it keeps finding more to sell on each one.

Section 5

Margin, cash and capital allocation

The margin arises from a cost base that is almost entirely fixed against a revenue base that is almost entirely variable. Network and processing expense was $894m in FY2025 against $40.0bn of net revenue — 2.2%. Carrying one more transaction across an already-built network costs close to nothing. The largest expense is people ($7.0bn against 34,100 employees), and people are added deliberately, not in proportion to volume.

Reported operating margin fell to 60.0% in FY2025 from 65.7%, and the cause is one line: the litigation provision was $2,562m against $462m, reflecting $2.2bn of additional accruals for the interchange multidistrict litigation. Excluding it, FY2025 operating margin was 66.4% — the highest in the ten-year series (inferred). The underlying margin did not deteriorate; a legal settlement was recognised.

The one thing that genuinely compresses the margin

Client incentives are the real margin variable and they have moved in one direction for a decade — 18.4% of gross revenue in FY2016, 25.8% in FY2021, 28.3% in FY2025. Roughly ten points of gross revenue given back over nine years; $15.8bn in FY2025 alone, close to four times the FY2016 figure.

The mechanism is the duopoly. Because Visa and Mastercard compete for individual bank portfolios rather than on system-wide price, the contest for a large issuer's credential is settled with an upfront payment and a volume-linked rebate, and each renewal resets the price. The consequence is that Visa's gross pricing power and its net pricing power are different things: it raises posted fees annually and hands a rising share of the proceeds back to keep the routing. That is why the take rate on payments volume has stayed near 0.29% while gross fees rose. Anyone modelling Visa's pricing power from posted rates alone will overstate it.

The structure that makes the litigation someone else's problem

Visa's most distinctive financial feature is not on the income statement. Under the US retrospective responsibility plan established at the 2008 IPO, monetary liabilities from the US covered interchange litigation are paid from a dedicated escrow, and the escrow is funded by a downward adjustment to the rate at which Class B common stock converts into Class A. The economic effect: the pre-IPO US bank owners, not Class A shareholders, absorb the cost of the interchange litigation. Visa states plainly that the FY2025 adjustments left diluted Class A earnings per share unchanged.

Section 6

Cyclicality, constraints and what to monitor

Visa's cyclicality is not what a first reading suggests. Because revenue is a function of consumer spending, the instinct is to treat it as cyclical. Its own filings say otherwise, and the pandemic is the cleanest natural experiment available for any company.

In the twelve months to 30 June 2020, total nominal payments volume was $8,713bn against $8,615bn — growth of 1%, through the sharpest contraction in modern consumer spending. Consumer credit volume fell 4%; consumer debit rose 7%; cash volume fell 8%. Visa FY2020 Form 10-K. Consumers stopped borrowing, kept spending, and abandoned cash faster than consumption.

The damage was located entirely in one line. International transaction revenue fell from $7,804m in FY2019 to $6,299m in FY2020, down 19%, and total net revenue fell 5%. The operating margin moved from 65.3% to 64.5% — barely at all, because the cost base did not need to change. Recovery took two full years: international transaction revenue was still below FY2019 in FY2021 at $6,530m, even as domestic volumes had normalised.

Mastercard's 2009 disclosure shows the same shape in a credit-led recession: worldwide gross dollar volume fell 3.3% in dollars but rose 1.4% in local currency, credit volume fell 8.8%, debit volume rose 10.3% and processed transactions grew 6.9%. Two different shocks, sixteen years apart, one answer. Domestic transaction count is close to recession-proof. Cross-border is where the cycle lives — it falls roughly 30% and takes about two years back.

Where the business sits now

At the June 2026 quarter every operating measure is at or above its prior peak. Payments volume, processed transactions and cross-border volume are at records; cross-border is growing 14–15%, above its own long-run trend, on travel and cross-border ecommerce; and the operating margin excluding litigation is at the top of its ten-year range. This is a peak reading, not a trough reading — and the distinction matters, because a record margin at a peak and a record margin at a trough are opposite facts.

The downside case is therefore specific rather than general. A travel contraction of the 2020 kind would remove roughly a fifth of international transaction revenue — about $2.8bn of gross revenue on the FY2025 base (inferred by applying the FY2020 decline to the FY2025 line) — against a cost base that would not fall. Domestic volume would be broadly unaffected. Margin would compress by low single digits, not by half. The reflex reading (consumer spending falls, therefore Visa falls proportionally) is not what the evidence shows.

Durable — likely intact in ten years

  • Per-transaction economics indifferent to the price level, with transaction count growing faster than value
  • Cash displacement: cash still ~52% of euro-area point-of-sale transactions by number
  • A cost base that does not grow with volume — network and processing expense at 2.2% of net revenue

Section 7

Risks, unknowns and questions for deeper work

These are the risks cyclicality does not capture — what would change the business rather than pause it.

  • Debit routing is the live structural threat, not interchange. Regulation II requires issuers to enable at least two unaffiliated networks, and since July 2023 that extends to card-not-present transactions. The DOJ alleges Visa processes more than 60% of US debit transactions, collects over $7bn a year in US debit fees, and preserves that position through agreements foreclosing at least 45% of debit volume; the court denied Visa's motion to dismiss in full on 23 June 2025. If those agreements unwind, merchants route to whichever network is cheaper on a transaction Visa already switches — which converts a fee Visa sets into a fee Visa must bid for. No trial date has been set (NOT FOUND).
  • A scheme-fee cap would be different in kind from every regulation Visa has absorbed. An interchange cap moves money from issuers to merchants and leaves Visa's line untouched — which is why the Fed measures US network fees flat at 0.28% of value across twelve years of Reg II. A scheme-fee cap would come out of Visa's revenue directly, with nothing to recoup it from.
  • Client concentration compounds the routing risk. One client accounted for 11% of total net revenue in each of FY2023, FY2024 and FY2025, and Visa notes clients generally retain flexibility to issue non-Visa products and may in certain circumstances terminate on relatively short notice without significant early termination fees. A single large renewal can move the incentive ratio, and the counterparty's alternative is a network of comparable capability. The client is not named (NOT FOUND).
  • Credit is protected today because a bank transfer is not a credit line — an argument about product, not about rails. UPI and Pix are both under a decade old and neither yet offers credit at scale. If credit-on-UPI or an equivalent achieves meaningful merchant adoption, the basis for treating credit as protected weakens considerably. No source settles how far that has progressed.
  • Stablecoins and agentic commerce are named by Visa and unquantifiable from any source. Its FY2025 10-K cites blockchain payments and stablecoins as gaining regulatory clarity, operating 24/7 and capable of supporting cross-border transactions — a description of exactly the highest-yielding line in the business. Marked unknown rather than dismissed, because the mechanism is coherent and the exposed revenue line is the valuable one.
  • Two decades of litigation have not stopped, and the disclosed exposure post-dates the annual report. $17.4bn of interchange at issue in unresolved US covered claims as of 11 May 2026; in Europe, more than 1,200 merchant claims since 2013 with over 950 settled. In February 2026 the UK Competition Appeal Tribunal found interchange was largely not passed on by merchants — a finding Visa is appealing and which, if it stands, affects the quantum of European claims. The retrospective responsibility plan absorbs US covered litigation through Class B dilution; it does not absorb the European exposure, and it does not absorb the cash.

What the sources could not answer

  • The revenue contribution of Prisma and Newpay — and therefore organic growth for FY2026.
  • Take rate by product. Visa does not split net revenue between credit, debit and commercial — the single most useful missing number, because the account-to-account evidence says the exposure is concentrated in debit.
  • Segment profitability of any kind. Visa reports as one operating segment, so the margin on value-added services versus the core switch is unknown — for 30% of revenue.
  • Visa Direct revenue, disclosed only as transaction counts and partner numbers.
  • The maintenance-versus-growth split of capital expenditure.
  • The identity of the client representing 11% of net revenue.
  • A trial date in United States v. Visa; and whether the Federal Reserve formally withdrew its October 2023 proposal to lower the debit interchange cap.

Three of these would need resolving before an investor could hold a firm view: the debit share of net revenue, which determines how much of the business is genuinely exposed to account-to-account rails rather than merely adjacent to them; the margin on value-added services, which determines whether the fastest-growing 30% of revenue is accretive or dilutive; and the DOJ outcome, which determines whether Visa continues to set the price of US debit switching.

Section 8

Investor takeaways

  • What this business really is: a toll on the movement of money that its owner neither lends nor underwrites — paid by both sides of its own platform, on a base of $13.9 trillion, at 0.288%.
  • The core economic engine: a fixed cost base against a variable revenue base. Network and processing expense is 2.2% of net revenue, so each additional transaction is close to pure margin — hence 66% operating margin before litigation and free cash flow at 54% of revenue.
  • The main growth lever: selling more onto transactions Visa already carries. Value-added services grew 33% in the June 2026 quarter to roughly 30% of revenue, requiring no new volume and facing no price regulation.
  • What could break the story: not disintermediation — account-to-account rails destroyed debit and cash in India and Brazil while credit accelerated — but the loss of pricing discretion. Debit routing and the DOJ case attack it directly; scheme-fee regulation attacks it prospectively; rising client incentives are already eroding it quietly, from 18% of gross revenue in FY2016 to 28% today.
  • What to monitor: the client incentive ratio each quarter, cross-border volume growth excluding intra-Europe, and the two regulatory files — the SDNY docket and the PSR and RBA scheme-fee reviews.
