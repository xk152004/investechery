NVIDIA is the stronger business for the next decade, and it is not close.
NVIDIA owns the platform the fastest-growing workload is written for and prices whole systems against it; Intel defends a mature CPU franchise whose margin is set by how full its factories are.

The Three Answers

1. Who has the stronger growth profile, by product x geography?
NVIDIA, clearly

Three NVIDIA cells carry the next five years. AI compute sold as rack-scale systems grows with customer capacity and with content per gigawatt, which management puts at about $18B for Hopper, $25B for Blackwell and $40B for Vera Rubin; its top five customers alone expect to spend nearly $800B of capex in 2026 and $1.3T in 2027 (Q2 FY2027 call). Demand is also widening: AI clouds, industrial and enterprise (ACIE) revenue grew 138% year on year against 102% for hyperscale (Q2 FY2027 10-Q). Networking (Spectrum-X up 2.6x) and CPUs (Grace and Vera above $5B trailing, expected to more than double in FY2028) ride the same racks (Q2 FY2027 call). Intel's 2026 rebound is price in a shortage: client ASPs rose 27% while units fell 8%, and server ASPs rose 48% on units up 9% (Q2 2026 10-Q), while AMD took 7.3 points of x86 server unit share in a year (Mercury Research via HotHardware, Aug 2026). Intel's one structural new engine, external foundry, billed $293M in Q2 2026 (Q2 2026 10-Q).

Revenue indexed to 100 at the first common fiscal year. Intel FY2021-FY2025 ($79.0B to $52.9B); NVIDIA FY2022-FY2026 ($26.9B to $215.9B), each fiscal year labeled by the calendar year it mostly covers. Sources: Intel 10-Ks FY2021, FY2023, FY2025; NVIDIA 10-Ks FY2022, FY2024, FY2026.
2. Who converts that growth into superior margins?
NVIDIA, clearly

NVIDIA prices a system against the value of the compute it delivers and buys wafer capacity from TSMC, so its costs flex with volume; the Compute & Networking segment earned 67.3% operating margin in FY2026 and 71.0% in Q2 FY2027 (FY2026 10-K; Q2 FY2027 10-Q). Intel's designer arm earned 25.9% in FY2025, and then its factory, which bills about 98% of its revenue to Intel itself, lost 57.9 cents per dollar (FY2025 10-K, Note 3). The same company wins growth and margin, so the tension in this duel is direction, not level: NVIDIA guides gross margin down to a 71-72% trough in Q4 FY2027 on memory costs before 72-73% in FY2028, while Intel guides Q3 2026 to 41.0% from 34.8% in FY2025 (Q2 FY2027 call; Intel Q2 2026 release, 23 Jul 2026). That narrows a 37-point gap by a few points; it does not close it.

Three-year averages of reported ratios: Intel FY2023-FY2025, NVIDIA FY2024-FY2026. Intel EBIT includes itemized restructuring ($7.0B in FY2024); excluding it the average is -3.0%. Latest half-year gross margin: Intel 39.9%, NVIDIA 75.0%. Sources: Intel FY2025 10-K and Q2 2026 10-Q; NVIDIA FY2026 10-K and Q2 FY2027 10-Q.
3. Where do the vulnerabilities sit if the tide turns?
Intel breaks first NVIDIA breaks harder

Intel's exposure is cost rigidity under borrowed pricing. Its 2026 margin recovery rests on shortage-era ASPs and on substrate and memory constraints it expects to persist "into next year" (Q2 2026 10-Q); when supply normalizes, AMD and Arm designers buy more TSMC capacity, prices reset as they did in FY2025, and fixed fab cost turns every price cut into lost margin. It starts from a thin cushion: FY2025 operating cash flow of $9.7B against $14.6B of capex (FY2025 10-K), bridged by a $20B equity raise in August 2026 (Intel release, 11 Aug 2026). NVIDIA's exposure is a commitment asymmetry: supply and capacity commitments rose from $119B to $279B in one quarter, plus up to $105B of lease guarantees for OpenAI's Ohio campus, while customers can cancel purchase orders "without penalty" (Q2 FY2027 10-Q; FY2026 10-K). Three direct customers took 44% of first-half revenue and design competing chips. Intel breaks first because it has almost no margin to lose; NVIDIA, with a 60% operating margin, can absorb a slowdown but not a pause that strands $279B of commitments (inferred).

Segment growth engines

AI compute (NVIDIA): volume of gigawatts deployed multiplied by revenue per gigawatt, which rises each generation because NVIDIA adds CPU, networking and now Groq inference hardware to the rack (Q2 FY2027 call). Management's preliminary FY2028 view is revenue growth of about 70%, with supply the bottleneck "at least through the end of fiscal year 2028" (Q2 FY2027 call). Growth is organic: the December 2025 Groq agreement bought a licence and staff, not revenue, and the $11.9B Hugging Face deal has not closed (FY2026 10-K; 8-K 3 Sep 2026).

Server CPU (both): this is the only cell where the two meet head on. Intel's DCAI revenue rose 59% in Q2 2026, mostly on ASP (Q2 2026 10-Q), and Intel is co-developing custom x86 data-center CPUs with NVLink for NVIDIA, which invested $5B in Intel at $23.28 a share in December 2025 (NVIDIA release, 18 Sep 2025; closing reported by Quartz). NVIDIA meanwhile sells its own Grace and Vera CPUs and says it sees about $20B of server-CPU demand (Q2 FY2027 call). Intel's fastest-growing cell increasingly lives inside a platform NVIDIA defines.

PC client (both): Intel ships about seven in ten x86 PC processors but AMD's client unit share rose to 30.3% and Arm-based chips to 15.3% (Mercury Research via HotHardware and The Register, Aug 2026). NVIDIA enters PC processors this fall with RTX Spark, an Arm chip co-designed with MediaTek, through Dell, HP, Lenovo and others (NVIDIA release, 31 May 2026).

Insight: The overlap cells, server and PC CPUs, are where Intel is largest and where NVIDIA is now entering with Arm designs. Implication: Intel's best growth cell faces a new entrant that also controls the accelerator the CPU must attach to. KPI: NVIDIA CPU revenue more than doubling in FY2028 (call guide) against Intel DCAI growth; Mercury Arm server share above 20% by 2Q 2027. [Q2 FY2027 call; Q2 2026 10-Q; Mercury Research via The Register]

Price control and route-to-market

Both sell on cancellable purchase orders through OEMs, ODMs and cloud providers, and both net rebates and channel incentives from revenue (FY2025 10-K; FY2026 10-K). The difference is who sets the price. NVIDIA is passing a memory cost spike through, with price increases executed for Q1 FY2028, and still guides gross margin to 72-73% (Q2 FY2027 call). Intel raised ASPs in 2026 only while "market demand exceeded our available product supply", and says pricing contributed "to a lesser extent" than mix (Q2 2026 10-Q); in FY2025, with supply adequate, server ASPs fell 4% "driven by a competitive environment" (FY2025 10-K).

Insight: NVIDIA realizes price through a cost shock; Intel realizes price only in a shortage. Implication: When supply normalizes, Intel's net price falls toward FY2025 levels while NVIDIA's holds within a few margin points. KPI: Intel server ASP change in the first quarter after Intel says shortages have eased; NVIDIA gross margin at or above 72% in FY2028. [Q2 2026 10-Q; FY2025 10-K; Q2 FY2027 call]

Competitive context

AI accelerators: GPUs are expected to be 69.7% of 2026 AI-server shipments and custom ASICs 27.8%, the highest ASIC share since 2023 (TrendForce, Jan 2026). Broadcom's AI semiconductor revenue was $16.7B in its latest quarter, up 221%, and AMD's data-center segment $6.7B, against NVIDIA's $89.0B of Data Center revenue (company releases, Aug-Sep 2026). NVIDIA is the incumbent, and custom silicon is the share-taker. x86 CPUs: AMD holds 34.5% of server and 30.3% of client units and 46.2% of server revenue in Q1 2026; Intel is the share donor (Mercury Research). Foundry: TSMC took 72.5% of top-10 foundry revenue in 2Q 2026; Intel Foundry is not ranked (TrendForce, Sep 2026).

Risks by segment

AI compute (NVIDIA exposed): hyperscale was 55% of Q2 FY2027 Data Center revenue, and those buyers are named competitors building their own chips (Q2 FY2027 10-Q; FY2026 10-K). Inference is the natural first workload to move, and every ASIC generation that clears "good enough" moves hyperscale volume off NVIDIA at the margin (inferred). PC and server CPUs (Intel exposed): each design win AMD or an Arm licensee takes is usually built at TSMC, so it removes Intel product revenue and fab loading at once, raising unit cost on what remains (inferred). Foundry (Intel exposed): 14A proceeds without a committed external customer; Intel said in January 2026 it expected customer decisions from the second half of 2026 (Tom's Hardware, Jan 2026).

NVIDIA has the higher-probability growth and margin path: its biggest cells sit in the fastest-growing profit pool and grow on content per gigawatt, while Intel's grow only when shortages let it raise price.
In compute, whoever owns the software the workload is written for sets the terms; NVIDIA owns it for AI, Intel for a shrinking share of general-purpose work.

Moats: what rivals cannot copy

Intel

x86 licence duopoly with AMD and the Windows and enterprise installed base: High durability in PCs and on-premise servers, Medium and eroding in cloud, where Arm reached 13.6% of server units (Mercury, Q2 2026). The only US company running leading-edge logic R&D and volume manufacturing, with the US government holding about 10%: Medium, a policy asset that does not yet earn a return (FY2025 10-K; 8-K 25 Aug 2025).

NVIDIA

CUDA plus "hundreds of" libraries and SDKs, built on more than $76.7B of cumulative R&D: High, since a rival needs years of developer adoption, not money (FY2026 10-K). Rack-scale co-design of GPU, CPU, NVLink and networking on an annual cadence, now in a third NVL72 generation: High (Q2 FY2027 call). Scale in networking, number one in data-center Ethernet switching: Medium-High (IDC 1Q26).

Insight: NVIDIA's moat protects the growing pool; Intel's protects a maturing one and is being bypassed by Arm in the cloud. Implication: Intel can defend PC and on-premise share but cannot use x86 to enter AI compute, as its own 10-K concedes. KPI: Arm server unit share (Mercury) above 20% by 2Q 2027 erodes Intel; ASIC share of AI-server shipments (TrendForce) above 35% in 2027 erodes NVIDIA. [FY2025 10-K; FY2026 10-K; Mercury; TrendForce]

Customers: who controls net price and access

Both depend on a few buyers. Intel's three largest customers took 43% of FY2025 revenue (19%, 12%, 12%) (FY2025 10-K); NVIDIA's three largest direct customers took 44% of first-half FY2027 revenue (16%, 15%, 13%), down from one customer alone at 22% in FY2026 (Q2 FY2027 10-Q; FY2026 10-K). The difference is in exercised pricing power: NVIDIA's buyers accept price increases while supply is short through FY2028; Intel's buyers are OEMs who can switch to AMD on the same socket economics, and did so as supply allowed. NVIDIA's weakness is that its biggest buyers are also named competitors.

Insight: Concentration is equal; price-setting is not. Implication: Margin pressure lands on Intel first when demand softens, and on NVIDIA only if the hyperscalers' own chips reach parity. KPI: NVIDIA largest direct customer share below 12% (diversifying) by FY2028; Intel top-three share of revenue and server ASP after the shortage. [FY2025 10-K; FY2026 10-K; Q2 FY2027 10-Q]

Suppliers: who absorbs shocks

Intel's integration gives it continuity on its own nodes but not on its best products, which rely on TSMC without a long-term contract, and its Intel 7 output in Israel is flagged as uninsured war exposure (FY2025 10-K; Q2 2026 10-Q). NVIDIA depends on three memory makers and on TSMC's CoWoS, and memory pricing has just cost it about three points of guided gross margin; its answer is scale, with $279B of commitments that make it the priority buyer (Q2 FY2027 10-Q and call). Pass-through is partial for both: Intel raised ASPs "in part to offset higher input costs", NVIDIA executed price increases. NVIDIA's floor after pass-through is a 71-72% gross margin; Intel's ceiling in a shortage is about 41%.

Insight: Both pass input costs through partially; NVIDIA does it from a far higher margin and with priority access bought by commitments. Implication: In a supply shock NVIDIA keeps shipping at stable net price; Intel keeps its own nodes running but not its TSMC-built products. KPI: NVIDIA gross margin versus its 71-72% Q4 FY2027 trough guide; Intel gross margin versus its 41% Q3 2026 guide. [Q2 FY2027 call; Intel Q2 2026 release]

The causal gap

Three causes explain most of the difference. First, platform ownership: CUDA against x86 in a maturing market (Major); Intel cannot close it, as the failure of its AI accelerators so far shows (FY2025 10-K). Second, asset-light versus fixed-cost manufacturing (Major); Intel can close it only by filling its fabs with outside customers, a 14A story whose volume ramp Intel itself dates to 2028 (Q2 2026 prepared remarks). Third, customer price-setting (Moderate); this flips only if hyperscaler ASICs reach parity. None of the three can be closed with money in 18 months.

NVIDIA holds the stronger power position on all three levers; the early warnings are NVIDIA gross margin below 70% without a mix reason, and Intel server unit share below 60%.
NVIDIA keeps twice the gross margin on four times the revenue; the gap is made of pricing power and a cost base that flexes, against a factory that does not.

The structural gap

Gross margin carries the most persistent gap: 32.7, 42.3 and 36.3 points across the three paired years, never below 30. The mechanism comes from both earlier lenses: NVIDIA prices a system against the value of AI compute and buys wafers as it needs them, while Intel prices CPUs against AMD in maturing markets and carries factory cost whether or not the nodes are full. That confirms the power map; the company with pricing power shows it in gross margin. The second gap is quieter and matters more for the next decade: NVIDIA's R&D is now larger in dollars ($18.5B vs $13.8B in the last full year, $13.4B vs $6.7B in the last half-year), so the roadmap budget runs against Intel, whose 17% R&D cut in FY2025 lifted margins by shrinking it (10-Ks; 10-Qs). Intel's opex cuts are real self-help, visible in a Q2 2026 operating margin of 11.1%, but they cannot touch a gap that sits in cost of sales.

NVIDIA runs the leaner engine by a wide and persistent margin, and the gap is structural, in cost of sales, not in overhead Intel could cut.

What would flip the call

  • Hyperscaler custom silicon takes inference. Hyperscale was 55% of NVIDIA's Q2 FY2027 Data Center revenue and those buyers design their own accelerators; Broadcom's AI revenue grew 221%. If ASIC share keeps rising, NVIDIA loses volume and then price in its biggest cell. Early warning: NVIDIA Hyperscale growth below Broadcom AI semiconductor growth for three straight quarters, with gross margin below 70% and no mix explanation (10-Qs; Broadcom releases).
  • A capex pause meets the commitment book. $279B of supply commitments ($92B due in the rest of FY2027) and up to $105B of OpenAI lease guarantees sit against cancellable orders; the $4.5B H20 charge showed how fast inventory turns into loss. Early warning: inventory provisions above 3% of revenue for two quarters, or any sequential Data Center decline (NVIDIA 10-Q).
  • Intel's foundry finds anchors and its fixed cost turns into leverage. A committed external 14A customer plus Apple and Microsoft ramps at 18A-P (both reported, neither confirmed) would give Intel the one physically scarce asset NVIDIA rents. Early warning: a named 14A customer by 30 Jun 2027 and an Intel Foundry quarterly operating loss below $1B (from $2.1B in Q2 2026) by Q4 2027 (Intel 8-K and 10-Q).

Where to spend your time
Spend the first hours on NVIDIA, not to confirm it is the stronger business, which the filings settle, but to stress-test the two things that could break it: the $279B commitment book and hyperscaler ASIC substitution.
