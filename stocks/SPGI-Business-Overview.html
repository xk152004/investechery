Business Overview

S&P Global Inc. (NYSE: SPGI)

23 September 2026 · Built from SPGI's 10-Ks FY2016–FY2025, 10-Qs and earnings calls through Q2 2026, and 8-Ks through July 2026, plus primary industry sources (SEC, ESMA, competitor filings). Not a valuation and not a recommendation.
S&P Global is paid to be the number other people's rules point to: the rating a bond index requires, the index an ETF tracks, the price a futures contract settles on.
The economic engine

The unit is one benchmark reference, sold more than once. In FY2025 Ratings billed more than $4.3tn of debt and booked $2.47bn of transaction fees, about 5–6 bps per dollar rated (inferred), then another $2.25bn of surveillance and relationship fees on ratings already outstanding, and licensed the same ratings to investors as data. The segment earned a 64% operating margin. Indices collects about 2.5 bps on ETF assets tracking its indices (inferred) at a 69% margin. Capex is about 1% of revenue, so most of the profit turns into cash.

2. What the company does

Capital markets need shared reference points. A pension fund cannot buy a bond without a rating its mandate recognises, an ETF sponsor needs an index investors trust, and an oil cargo needs a price both sides accept. S&P Global supplies those reference points. In FY2025 it earned $15.3bn of revenue and $6.5bn of GAAP operating profit, a 42.2% margin (FY2025 10-K). Management counts Ratings, Indices, Platts and ratings data sold through Market Intelligence as "benchmark businesses": nearly two-thirds of revenue and more than 80% of operating profit (Q2 2026 call).

Ratings shows the unit most clearly. An issuer pays a fee when a bond is rated, then annual surveillance and relationship fees while it is outstanding. The same rating is licensed again to investors through Market Intelligence products such as RatingsDirect, for which Market Intelligence paid Ratings a $172m internal royalty in FY2025. One piece of analytical work is monetised at issuance, over the life of the debt, and again as data. Indices works the same way: asset-linked fees of $1.21bn in FY2025 on average ETF AUM of $4.77tn, plus exchange royalties including a $193m share of CME Group's equity-index profits (FY2025 10-K).

3. Industry, competitive position & moat

Each core market sells the right to be the accepted reference, and the profit goes to whoever others are obliged or accustomed to cite. Capital needs are small; the barrier is acceptance, and acceptance is built by third parties writing the benchmark into their own rules.

Ratings: an oligopoly held in place by index rules

Ten firms are SEC-registered NRSROs, but S&P, Moody's and Fitch held 93.4% of outstanding ratings at end-2024 (SEC OCR 2026 report); S&P alone had 50.4% of EU agency revenue (ESMA 2025). Dodd-Frank removed ratings from federal rules without reducing concentration, because the binding lock is private: the Bloomberg US Aggregate Index decides eligibility using the middle rating of Moody's, S&P and Fitch, and KBRA and DBRS told the SEC that index-inclusion rules hold back their growth. Moody's rating agency earned a 60.1% adjusted margin in FY2024 against 63% for S&P Ratings, which suggests the incumbents share the profit pool rather than compete on price. The edges are visible, though: Morningstar DBRS grew ratings revenue 22% in 2025 from a small base, and smaller agencies are strongest in structured finance and private credit.

Indices and Platts: embedded in other people's products

About $7.2tn was benchmarked to the S&P 500 at end-2024 (S&P DJI survey). ETFs, CME's E-mini futures and listed options all reference it, so replacing the index means rebuilding a liquid market. Fee pressure has limits: Vanguard's 2012 switch from MSCI to CRSP is the main precedent, and self-indexed funds, now about 20% of broad-equity ETFs, charge more than externally indexed peers (academic study, 2025). Platts assessments settle futures on ICE, CME and SGX. Regulators made SPGI sell OPIS for $1.15bn before approving the IHS Markit deal because they saw Platts and OPIS as close substitutes (DOJ 2021), which confirms that these benchmarks form a concentrated market.

Market Intelligence: a good data business, not a benchmark

Market Intelligence competes on ordinary terms with Bloomberg, LSEG and FactSet, where organic growth is mid-single-digit (LSEG Data & Analytics +4.5% in FY2024; FactSet ASV +5.7% in FY2025). Its 36% adjusted margin and ~6% ACV growth put it squarely among its peers. Winners in this industry own a reference others have written into their contracts; SPGI is that kind of company in Ratings, Indices and Platts, and an ordinary strong vendor in Market Intelligence.

4. Growth engine

Revenue rose from $6.1bn in FY2017 to $15.3bn in FY2025, but most of the step came from IHS Markit (+35% in FY2022). Before the merger revenue compounded at 8.2% a year (FY2017–21); after it, at 10.8% (FY2023–25), helped by the Ratings recovery. The 10-Ks do not disclose organic growth; the calls show it running close to reported recently.

5. Margin, cash & capital allocation

Costs are mostly people and technology and do not move with volume. When issuance collapsed in 2022, Ratings lost $1.05bn of revenue and $0.96bn of operating profit, so about 90% of the lost revenue came out of profit (inferred). The same leverage has run upward since: Ratings' margin went from 56% in 2023 to 64% in 2025 and 68% in Q2 2026. Indices has held between 66% and 70% throughout, so Ratings carries the company's operating leverage. GAAP margins understate cash earnings by about 7 points because of $1.07bn a year of amortisation from the IHS Markit merger; the adjusted margin was 54.3% in Q2 2026.

Over FY2016–FY2025 the company spent about $29.8bn on buybacks and $7.7bn on dividends, against about $1.1bn of capex. IHS Markit was paid in shares (113.8m issued); buybacks have since cut the count to 299m, still 24% above the pre-merger 241m. The 2026 plan is more aggressive: buybacks of more than $7bn, funded partly by a ~$2bn dividend from Mobility Global and $2bn of new debt, taking gross leverage to 2.7–2.8x at year-end, above the 2.0–2.5x target until end-2027 (Q2 2026 call). Buybacks are the default use of cash; M&A reshapes the portfolio; capex barely registers.

6. Cyclicality & where the cycle stands

The cyclical exposure is concentrated in Ratings transaction revenue. In 2022 global corporate bond issuance fell 24%, transaction revenue fell 45% and the Ratings margin dropped from 64% to 55%, while non-transaction revenue slipped only 2%. Indices moves with equity markets through asset-linked fees. Energy is subscription-based but slowed to 3% growth in Q2 2026 under sanctions, the Iran conflict and upstream consolidation.

Both cyclical engines are at or above prior peaks. Transaction revenue of $2.47bn in FY2025 was 10% above 2021 and grew another 25% in Q2 2026; the 68% quarterly Ratings margin exceeds the 2021 peak; ETF AUM is a record. Management points to about $11tn of rated debt maturing over four and a half years as evidence that volume is not being pulled forward. That supports volume. It does not protect today's margin if issuance returns to trend: a 2022-style halving of transaction revenue would remove more than $1bn of revenue at close to full profit (inferred).

7. Risks & open questions

  • AI weakens the least-protected half of Market Intelligence. The Platforms business (Capital IQ, Visible Alpha, With Intelligence) grows in the low single digits organically, and large-client renewals have lengthened as S&P negotiates AI and data-licensing terms. If clients reach S&P data through third-party AI tools, the desktop seat, where the pricing power sits, could shrink toward a cheaper feed. Clients are also "paying more attention to token costs" (Q2 2026 call).
  • Private credit shifts share at the margin. The fastest-growing ratings area is where smaller agencies already compete, and the Egan-Jones controversy has drawn Senate attention to standards; new rules could touch all agencies.
  • An index rule change hits the lock directly. If bond-index providers admitted other agencies, the main barrier would fall without any regulator acting.
  • Leverage and a peak-cycle buyback compound. A 2022-style downturn would cut EBITDA while leverage sits at 2.7–2.8x.
  • The CME put is a contingent cash call. CME Group can require SPGI to buy at least 20% of its 27% Indices stake at fair value; the redeemable interest is carried at $4.9bn.
  • Litigation follows credit losses. About $1.6bn was paid in 2015–16 over pre-crisis ratings; such liability arrives in downturns, not against them.
What the sources could not answer: ratings fee levels and price increases; full-year FY2025 organic growth and recast ex-Mobility financials; Platts' share versus Argus; Market Intelligence retention and desktop size; the dollar size of private-credit ratings; May 2026 Investor Day targets. The key question for deeper work is what Ratings earns in a normal issuance year, and whether Market Intelligence renewals hold price as AI distribution spreads.

8. Investor takeaways

  • What it is: a toll collector on financial reference points, with a solid but ordinary data business attached.
  • The engine: one product monetised repeatedly at 64–69% segment margins with capex near 1% of revenue.
  • The growth lever: debt issuance and index-linked assets, both at records, on top of mid-single-digit subscription growth.
  • What could break it: index or mandate rules that stop requiring Big-3 ratings, or AI turning the Market Intelligence desktop into a cheaper feed, while leverage is above target.
  • What to monitor: Ratings transaction revenue against its 2021 peak, Market Intelligence ACV and retention, and the Big-3 share in the SEC's NRSRO report.
